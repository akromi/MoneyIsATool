# Money Is a Tool — teacher guide

The calculators, the planners, and the Canadian Investment Challenge: what each
one is for, what a student actually does with it, and where it fits in a lesson.

Written for a teacher who has not seen any of it before. Nothing here needs a
finance background, and nothing here needs an account to try.

## 1. What these are, and what they are not

Every tool in this guide exists to make one decision visible. Not to give an
answer — to show the payment, the cost, the timeline and the tradeoff clearly
enough that a student can reason about them out loud.

That matters for how you use them. A calculator that produces a number is a
prompt for a conversation, not the end of one. The most useful question in every
lesson below is the same: *what would have to be true for this to be a good idea?*

**What they are not:**

- **Not financial advice.** Nothing recommends a product, a lender, an
  investment or a course of action. Every page says so.
- **Not a record system.** The calculators and planners save nothing to any
  server. Numbers live in the browser on the device they were typed into.
- **Not assessment tools on their own.** They produce a printable report; the
  marking comes from the reasoning, and section 6 is about that.

**What they cost:** the calculators and planners are free and need no account.
The Challenge needs a school licence.

## 2. Before the first lesson

**The address is `moneyisatool.ca`.** One page. No sign-in, no download, no
class setup. A student opens it and starts typing.

**Nothing is sent anywhere.** Everything is calculated on the device and saved
only in that browser. This is worth stating to a class out loud, because
students who believe a page is recording their family's finances will type
nonsense into it, and you will have taught nothing.

**It works offline.** Once loaded, it keeps working without a connection, and on
most phones it can be added to the home screen like an app. Useful where the
school network is unreliable.

**Work is saved per device and per browser.** A student who starts on a school
computer and finishes on their phone starts again. Plan for one device per
student for the whole activity, or have them print at the end of each lesson.

**Everything prints.** Each calculator and planner has a **Save as PDF report**
button, which prints a clean copy with the empty rows dropped. That printed
report is what gets handed in, marked or taken home. It is also the only way
work survives a shared computer.

**A caution worth giving explicitly.** Some of these tools invite real household
numbers. Real numbers make the lesson land, and they also make a student's
family finances visible to whoever is sitting next to them. Offer a fictional
household as a first-class option, not a fallback for the shy. Every example in
this guide works with invented figures.

## 3. The four calculators

### My Household Budget

**What it does.** One group for income, and ten for spending — housing,
utilities and communications, food, transportation, children and education,
health and insurance, debt payments, savings and investing, personal and
leisure, and miscellaneous. It shows what is left after the plan, and includes a
Pay Period Check-In for a single payday rather than a whole month.

**What a student does.** Fills in the groups that apply to their household, real
or invented, and reads the figure at the bottom. Then changes one thing and
watches what moves.

**Where it fits.** The first lesson of a unit. It is the only tool here that
shows the whole picture, and every other tool is a zoom into one part of it.

**Watch for:** a budget that balances exactly to zero is usually a student
working backwards to make it come out neat. The interesting budgets are the ones
that do not balance — that is where the decision is.

**One activity.** Give the whole class the same fictional household and the same
income. Have them each build the budget, then compare: the spread between
students on identical inputs is the lesson. Ask the two students furthest apart
to explain their reasoning to each other.

### My Borrowing & Payment Calculator

**What it does.** Four things, in four sections: what a loan really costs;
comparing two offers on the same basis; whether a sale was really a sale; and
whether a payment fits a life.

**What a student does.** Enters an amount, a rate and a term, and sees the
payment and the total cost of borrowing side by side. Then changes the term and
watches the payment fall while the total cost rises.

**Where it fits.** Any lesson about credit, cars, phones, or "no payments for
twelve months". The comparison section is the strongest part: two offers that
look similar on the advertised monthly payment and are not similar at all.

**Watch for:** students anchoring on the monthly payment. That is exactly the
habit the tool exists to interrupt, so name it when you see it.

**One activity.** Find two real advertisements for the same car, phone or
furniture with different financing. Put both into the comparison. The question
is not which is cheaper — it is what the seller was hoping you would look at.

### My Savings & Goal Calculator

**What it does.** Two directions. Forward: what a regular contribution may grow
to over a period. Backward: what it takes each month to reach a target by a
date.

**What a student does.** Sets a goal they actually want — a trip, a car, a first
month's rent — and finds the monthly number. Then moves the date and watches the
number change.

**Where it fits.** Straight after borrowing, as the mirror image. The same
arithmetic that makes a loan expensive makes saving effective.

**Watch for:** growth rates entered optimistically. Ask where the number came
from. "What return did you assume, and why that one?" is a better question than
anything the calculator answers.

**One activity.** Two students, same goal, same target date, different start
dates — one starts now, one starts in three years. Both reach the goal. Compare
what each had to put aside. That single comparison does more for compound growth
than any formula.

### My Income & Tax Planning Calculator

**What it does.** Estimates what may happen before tax time: combining income
from more than one job, testing what an RRSP deduction changes, and working out
what to set aside from self-employment or side income.

**What a student does.** Enters income from one or more sources and sees an
estimate of tax owed, then tests a deduction against it.

**Where it fits.** A senior class, or any group with part-time jobs. The
two-jobs section answers a question that genuinely catches working teenagers:
why each employer withheld the right amount and money is still owed in April.

**Watch for:** this is an estimate, and it says so on the page. Rates, limits and
eligibility change. Treat every output as a prompt to check an official source,
never as an answer to file anything on.

**One activity.** A student with two part-time jobs, each withholding correctly
on its own. Show the shortfall. Then ask what they would have to do differently
in September to avoid it in April.

## 4. The three planners

The calculators answer questions that have a number at the end. The planners
handle the decisions that do not — where the work is asking the right question,
writing down the answer, and being able to look at it again in six months.

Each planner is a set of worksheet sections plus a **My decision** page where
the student records what they concluded and what they will do next. That last
page is usually the part worth marking.

### My Income & Tax Planner

**Sections:** what my income picture looks like; what I should check before tax
time; my decision.

**What it is for.** Gathering every source of income and what has been withheld
against it, then turning that into a short list of things to verify — RRSP room,
deductions and credits to look into, whether more than one employer is
withholding, whether side income needs a set-aside, whether income changes
affect benefits, and whether the paperwork is organised.

**Pairs with:** the Income & Tax calculator. The calculator estimates what may be
owed; the planner records what to check and decide. Most people want the
calculator first and the planner immediately after.

### My Home Decision Planner

**Sections:** what each option actually costs; does the option fit the life I
want; my decision.

**What it is for.** Setting up to three housing options side by side — renting,
buying, staying put, moving in with someone — and comparing full monthly cost and
cash needed up front, not the headline rent or the mortgage payment. Then a
second pass on the things a payment cannot answer: commute, flexibility, whether
the decision can be reversed, what it costs to leave.

**Where it fits.** A unit on independent living, or a graduating class. It is
the tool that most reliably surprises students: the gap between "rent" and "cost
of renting" is usually larger than they expect, in both directions.

### My Household Protection Planner

**Sections:** where my household is exposed; what protection I already have; my
protection priorities.

**What it is for.** Listing what could go wrong, listing what is already in place
against it, and turning the gap between the two into questions to ask. It is
explicitly not an insurance recommendation, and nothing is sold anywhere on the
page.

**Where it fits.** Late in a unit, once students have a budget to protect. It
works well as a conversation to have with a parent or guardian at home — the
printed report is designed to be taken to that conversation rather than handed
in.

**A note on tone.** This planner touches illness, death and job loss. Set it up
deliberately, offer the fictional household plainly, and let students opt out of
sharing anything they write.

## 5. The Canadian Investment Challenge

A simulation with virtual money, at `app.moneyisatool.ca`, included with a school
licence. Each student gets a fictional household and an amount to invest across
five Canadian securities — two dividend-paying shares, a growth share, a broad
index ETF and a bond ETF — chosen so that diversification and income are visible
choices rather than theory.

**Every trade asks why.** The reason is recorded with the trade, in the student's
own words, at the moment they made it. That record, not the portfolio value, is
the point of the whole exercise.

**Students have no accounts.** No email address, no sign-up. You create the
roster with names; each student gets a passcode you hand out; they sign in with
the class code, their name and that passcode. Holding no direct identifier is
what keeps this simple under Canadian school privacy rules.

**You enter the prices.** One closing price per instrument per trading day, read
off whatever source you already use and typed in — about a minute a day. This is
deliberate: an automatic feed of exchange prices needs the exchange's
authorisation, while a person typing a number they can see does not. Miss a day
and nothing breaks; prices simply do not move.

**One close per day, not live trading.** A simulation that rewards watching a
screen all day teaches watching a screen all day.

**The leaderboard starts hidden**, and there are good reasons to leave it that
way. A visible ranking changes behaviour immediately and predictably: students
who are behind take bigger risks to catch up. You always see the full ranking
yourself; the setting only governs what reaches a student.

**Full instructions live in the site**, under *Running the Challenge* on the
teacher pages — how to set a class up, what to hand out, what to do when a
passcode is lost, and a short answer for a principal asking what is held about
their students. That page is kept current; this section is an overview.

## 6. Marking a decision, not a result

The hardest thing about assessing any of this is that the student who did the
best thinking may not have the best number. In the Challenge, two students can
make identical, well-reasoned choices and finish a month apart on luck alone. In
the budget, the neatest sheet is often the least honest one.

So mark the reasoning. Four things are visible in the printed reports and the
trade records, and all four are defensible to a parent asking why a mark is what
it is:

| What to look for | Where you see it |
| --- | --- |
| Did they state an assumption, and is it plausible? | Growth rates, income figures, the fictional household details |
| Did the reasoning change as the numbers did? | The **My decision** pages; the reasons attached to later trades |
| Did they compare like with like? | The borrowing comparison; the housing options side by side |
| Can they say what would change their mind? | The decision pages, and any reflection you set alongside |

A student who writes "I am not sure yet" honestly, six times, is telling you
something a portfolio value cannot — and is often further along than the one
with a confident answer and no reason behind it.

## 7. Questions you will be asked

**"Is my data safe?"** The calculators and planners send nothing anywhere; the
numbers stay in the browser on the device. The Challenge holds a name, a class, a
hashed passcode, and the trades — no email address, no student number, no
tracking. It is hosted in Canada. Using initials or first names on the roster
means no direct identifier is held at all, and everything still works.

**"Do students need accounts?"** Not for the calculators or planners. Not for the
Challenge either — you create the roster, and they sign in with a class code, a
name and a passcode.

**"Does it work on phones?"** Yes, all of it. The calculators can be added to a
phone's home screen and used offline.

**"Can they cheat the Challenge?"** They can trade badly on purpose, which is
visible. They cannot get a better price than anyone else: every student trades
against the same closing price, the one you entered, and the ledger cannot be
edited afterwards — corrections are offsetting trades, in the open.

**"Is this investment advice?"** No, and every page says so. The instrument cards
explain how each thing works and what can go wrong with it; none of them says
buy.

**"What if I get a price wrong?"** Enter the right one for the same day and
portfolios revalue. Trades already placed keep the price they were made at, which
is the point of an unalterable record. A price more than half away from the last
one is refused until you confirm it, which catches the misplaced decimal point
before it does damage.

**"How long does a unit take?"** The budget is one lesson. Each other calculator
is a lesson or a homework. The planners are a lesson each plus a conversation at
home. The Challenge runs for weeks — six to eight is common — with about a minute
a day of your time.
